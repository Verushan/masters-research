Cooking With Strangers

We train agents to cook together in Overcooked, then test them with partners they have never met. Last week we found the reward could be cheated and fixed it. This week the full benchmark ran on the fixed reward, at six runs per method, on every kitchen: our method does not beat the hand-tuned baseline. We found three specific, mechanical causes and fixed them. The corrected agents close about half the gap at stage one, and at stage two the MORL agent is the best of its kind we have trained — its mean is not significantly ahead, but its worst run is seventy points better than the baseline's. No single agent we have trained beats the plain hand-shaped one.

If you have ten minutes

The whole story, in the order it happened

Seven beats. Each has the finding, then a line you can say more or less as written. Everything after this section is the evidence for these seven things.

We were measuring the wrong thing, and the agents noticed before we did

One of our four objectives rewarded passing items to your partner. Passing an item back and forth is free and repeatable, so agents learned to do that forever and cook nothing. Six separate training runs found this by themselves.

"Our coordination reward could be farmed. Agents shuttled one onion back and forth and scored beautifully while delivering no food at all. We found it, fixed it by only paying for a handoff once the item is actually used, and re-ran everything."

Evidence: the main result section, F1

Every earlier comparison was therefore meaningless — so we redid all of it

Every MORL number before mid-September was measured on the cheatable reward. We re-ran the full benchmark on the fixed one: four kitchens, six training runs per method, testing against sixteen partners each agent had never met.

"Everything I showed you before was measuring reward hacking rather than the method. So I threw the comparison away and re-ran it properly — four maps, six runs per method, and a significance test that uses the training run as the sample rather than the test partner, which was a second thing I'd been doing wrong."

Evidence: F2, F5

Done properly, our method loses

The hand-tuned baseline beats MORL on every kitchen where agents can cook — by 35 and 66 points on the two that discriminate, both statistically significant. This is the headline and it goes against us.

"The honest headline is that with a reward that can't be cheated, the hand-tuned baseline beats our method on every map where the agents can actually cook. I'd rather lead with that than bury it."

Evidence: F2

The reason was three specific bookkeeping errors in our reward — not the idea

Our reward paid for prep and plating right to the end of training, where the baseline's hints fade out and leave only deliveries. It paid a delivery to whichever cook carried it, where the baseline pays both. And the agent had no input describing what its partner had been doing.

"We found three concrete faults. Ours kept paying for chopping and plating forever, where the baseline stops and leaves just 'serve food'. Ours paid the cook who carried the plate, where the baseline pays both — so once the hints faded, nobody was paid to help their partner. And the agent had nothing in its input saying what the partner had been doing. Fixing the second one alone was worth fifty-seven points."

Evidence: the three causes section, F11

Two of the three fixes worked; the third idea turned out to be dead

The reward fixes closed about half the gap. Giving the agent a description of its partner's work never helped — in six separate tests, across two rewards, two kitchens and both training stages.

"The two reward fixes closed about half the gap. The partner-awareness idea simply doesn't work — I tested it six different ways, including the one setup where it had every reason to, and it was never better than not having it. I'd call that line closed."

Evidence: F11's correction table, F12

In the setting the proposal actually described, our method comes out best — but underpowered

Trained against a population of eighteen different partners rather than a copy of itself, the corrected MORL agent beats the standard one by 17 points on average, and its worst run is 70 points better. At six runs per method, neither gap is statistically significant.

"In the two-stage setup — where the agent trains against many different partners, which is what the proposal always described — our corrected agent is the best one we've trained. Seventeen points above the standard version on average, and its worst run is seventy points better. I want to be careful: at six runs neither of those is significant. One test said the consistency result was, but it relies on an assumption six runs can't support, so I'm not claiming it."

Evidence: F12

The pattern across everything: MORL doesn't raise the ceiling, it raises the floor

In every comparison in this project, MORL's average is level or behind and its worst-case and run-to-run spread are better. That is the claim the evidence supports, and it is a different claim from the one we set out to make.

"If there's a thesis-level claim here it's about reliability rather than peak performance — our method's worst runs are consistently much better, its best runs aren't. For something you'd eventually pair with a human, the worst case is arguably what matters, but I'd want more runs before leaning on it."

Evidence: F2, F8, F12

If they push

Questions they are likely to ask

The honest answer to each, including where the answer is "we don't know".

"So has this been six months of negative results?"
No — it has been the discovery that the previous results weren't measuring the method at all. Four things were broken in the measurement apparatus (a forgeable objective, a yardstick capturing 7% of the truth, a significance test on the wrong sample, and analysis code silently dropping half the runs). All four are fixed and documented, and each is a methods contribution in its own right. The performance question only became answerable once they were.
"Why should we believe the reliability claim if it isn't significant?"
You shouldn't, yet — that is why it is labelled directional. What supports it is the consistency of the direction rather than any single test: MORL's spread is lower than the baseline's in essentially every arm across the whole project, and at stage two the worst run differs by 70 points. Six more runs per arm would settle it, and that is running now.
"Isn't the fix just making your reward identical to the hand-tuned one?"
Fair challenge, and partly yes: annealing the dense terms and crediting deliveries to the team are both things the hand-tuned reward already did. The difference is that we arrived at them from a stated principle rather than by tuning — and the objective-design rule (an objective is forgeable when its trigger is reversible at no cost) predicts which objectives break before any training is run. That rule is the transferable part.
"Why not just use the hand-tuned reward, then?"
On these maps you could. The argument for the objective-vector approach was never that it beats a well-tuned reward on a map someone has already tuned — it is that it can be specified without that tuning. Our results neither confirm nor refute that, because we only tested maps where a tuned reward already exists.
"Does any of this say something about the benchmark itself?"
Yes, and it may be the most reusable finding. The best single agent on our main map is the plain hand-shaped stage-one agent. No stage-two agent — ours or ZSC-Eval's own pipeline — has beaten it in any arm we have run. The population pipeline is not obviously buying what it is assumed to buy.
"What would you do with another three months?"
Power the reliability result properly, and test the objective-vector claim where it is actually supposed to pay: a map with no hand-tuned reward available. Everything in this report tests our method on ZSC-Eval's home turf, which is the hardest place for it to win.

Start here

The problem in one minute

Overcooked is a two-player cooking game. Players fetch onions, put them in a pot, wait for the soup, plate it, and deliver it. Doing that well needs coordination — if both players fetch onions and nobody plates, nothing gets served.

Training two agents together is easy. They learn a private routine that works beautifully with each other, and falls apart with anyone else, rather like two people who have shared a kitchen for years and communicate in grunts. The research problem is zero-shot coordination: building an agent that works well with a partner it has never trained with.

Our proposed approach is multi-objective reinforcement learning. Instead of giving the agent one score to maximise, we give it several — deliveries, ingredient prep, plating, coordination — and a set of weights saying how much each matters. The hope is that an agent which can shift its priorities will adapt to whoever it is paired with.

The supervisors' concern, which motivated this work, was that hand-tuned rewards were performing so close to our method that the method looked unnecessary. Last week we showed that comparison was not measuring what we thought, because the reward could be cheated. This week's update is the comparison done properly — and it comes out against us. The rest of this report says where, by how much, why, and what we are changing.

Reference

The words we use

Everything below is defined here first, so no term in this report is load-bearing and unexplained.

Zero-shot coordination ZSC
Playing well with a partner you have never trained against. The whole point of the project — an agent that only works with itself has solved nothing.
Self-play
Training an agent by pairing it with a copy of itself. Cheap and effective, but it produces private conventions that do not transfer.
Cross-play
Evaluation where every agent is paired with every other. This is where zero-shot ability actually shows up, and it is the number that matters.
Held-out partner
A partner agent kept aside and never trained against, used only for testing. We use sixteen of them per kitchen, each with deliberately different habits.
Reward shaping
Hand-written bonuses ("+3 for putting an onion in a pot") added to guide learning. Effective, but tuned by hand per kitchen, and it bakes in the answer.
Objective
One component of our reward vector — deliveries, ingredient prep, plating, or coordination. Each counts events the game already tracks.
Preference weights w
How much the agent cares about each objective. Fixed weights stay put; adaptive weights shift during play, which is the mechanism meant to produce adaptability.
Seed
One training run. Runs differ by random chance, so a claim needs several seeds — a difference between two single runs usually means nothing.
Best response BR
An agent trained to work with one specific partner and nobody else. It shows the best score that partnership can reach, so it tells us how much a method is leaving on the table.
Layout / kitchen
The map. random0 has a counter dividing the two cooks, forcing handoffs. unident_s is more open. Different kitchens demand different teamwork.

The kitchens, and why the layout decides everything

Every number in this report is tied to a specific kitchen, and the kitchens demand genuinely different teamwork. These are drawn from the actual map files.

That last point is worth dwelling on, because it explains a result that looked like a bug. Our coordination objective counts items passed across a counter. On unident_s it scores exactly zero for every method we have ever run — not because the agents fail to cooperate, but because the map has no dividing counter, so there is never any need to hand anything over. The two cooks simply work around each other.

That matters for the adaptive-weights method specifically. Its weights drift toward whichever objective is being under-served — and an objective that is structurally impossible looks permanently under-served, so weight piles onto it and starves the objectives that can actually be achieved. We have now removed it on that map, and one of the running experiments tests whether that alone repairs the adaptive method.

The main result

The reward could be cheated, and the agents found out

One of our four objectives, coordination, counted handoffs: passing an item to your partner across a counter. It seemed like the obvious way to measure teamwork.

It is also trivially cheatable. Putting an item down and picking it up again are exact opposites, so two agents can pass one onion back and forth forever, scoring "coordination" the entire time and never cooking anything. They did. Six independent training runs discovered this on their own.

coordination 3.69×
ingredient prep 0.18×
plating 0.03×
deliveries 0.00×

How much of each objective an agent can score while delivering no food at all, relative to what agents earn when they do cook. The thin line marks 1.0 — above it, the objective is better cheated than earned. Measured over 21 successful and 9 failed training runs on random0.

The fix is not to delete the objective — coordination is the thing we care most about. It is to anchor it: a handoff now scores only once the receiving agent actually uses the item (puts the onion in a pot, delivers the soup), and scores nothing if they just put it back down.

The same training run, same random seed, same settings — only the objective definition changed.

Coordination did not die — it settled at 84.2, right in the range agents reach honestly. The objective still measures teamwork; it just can no longer be forged.

How to say it

"We were rewarding agents for passing items back and forth, and some of them figured out they could do that forever without ever cooking. We changed it so a handoff only counts once the item is actually used. The same run went from zero food delivered to nearly two hundred."

Why this generalises beyond our project

There is a rule here, and it predicted which objective would break before we looked at any results: an objective is cheatable when the action that triggers it can be undone at no cost. Putting an item down and picking it up cancel out. Potting an onion consumes it. Delivering a soup ends it. The three anchored objectives scored 0.18, 0.03 and 0.00; the reversible one scored 3.69.

That gives a way to design reward objectives that does not require knowing the answer in advance — which is exactly what hand-tuned reward shaping cannot offer.

F3

Knowing who your partner is does not help

Established

We ran a clean test of the adaptation idea. Three otherwise-identical agents: one told nothing about its partner, one told the partner's identity as a number, one told it as a proper category label. Then we scored each against the partners it trained with, so the identity information was genuinely useful.

Two conclusions. First, telling the agent who its partner is buys nothing — the control wins on one kitchen and ties on the other. Since being told is worth nothing, inferring it cannot be worth anything either. That puts a ceiling of roughly zero on this whole line of attack.

How to say it

"We gave an agent its partner's identity outright, which is the best case any adaptation method could hope for. It didn't help on either kitchen. So adaptation based on who the partner is looks like a dead end — which pushes us toward reacting to what the partner is doing instead."

F4

Our "how much are we leaving on the table" measure was badly wrong

Corrected

To say how good a score is, you need to know what score was achievable with that partner. We had been approximating that with "the best any agent in our collection happened to manage" — but none of those agents were trying to suit that partner, so it was an underestimate.

So we trained a genuine best response for each partner: 48 dedicated agents across three kitchens, each trained against one partner and nobody else. The old estimate turned out to be wildly off.

This reverses a conclusion I previously reported. I had written off random3 as a kitchen where no method can do anything. In fact there is a great deal to be won there — every one of our methods is simply capturing almost none of it. That is a far more interesting statement, and it makes random3 our most discriminating test rather than one to discard.

The error was also uneven, not a constant factor. That matters because it scrambles which partners look hardest — and "performance against your worst partners" is one of the headline robustness numbers.

How to say it

"We were judging our scores against a bad yardstick. We built a proper one by training a specialist agent for each partner. On one kitchen our old yardstick was capturing only 7% of what was actually achievable — so a layout I'd written off as hopeless is actually one where everything we've tried is failing badly. That's a much more useful result."

F5

A statistical error was inflating our confidence

Corrected

We had reported a result at p = 0.034 — conventionally "significant". That test compared methods across the 16 partners, treating each partner as an independent data point. But the partners are the same fixed test set for every method; what actually varies between methods is the training run. We had three runs.

Re-tested properly, the same difference gives p = 0.200 — not significant. Worse, with only three runs per method the test cannot mathematically produce a p-value below 0.10, so significance was unreachable in principle rather than merely unattained. We now run six per method, which makes the test capable of a real answer.

How to say it

"One of our earlier significance results was measured the wrong way — we treated test partners as our sample size when the real sample size is the number of training runs. Corrected, it isn't significant. We've doubled the runs so the test can actually decide."

F6

The multi-ingredient kitchens did not pose the problem we adopted them for

Corrected

You suggested richer kitchens with multiple ingredients, where two cooks might genuinely divide the work — one on onions, one on tomatoes. We checked the built-in multi-ingredient maps before committing to them, and found the recipes are priced so that an onions-only strategy already earns the maximum. Tomatoes are optional, cost extra walking, and buy nothing.

So those maps posed exactly the same undivided task as the simple ones — which is precisely the condition under which no division of labour is expected, and none was found.

We built re-priced versions (identical maps, only the recipe values changed) where mixed recipes pay three times as much per unit of cooking time. Partner training there did show agents using both ingredients, and one pair split into a prep role and a delivery role. But the re-priced kitchen turned out to be too hard to learn at all in our training budget: half the baseline runs, a third of the MORL runs, and the partners themselves all score near zero. It cannot separate methods.

The original multi-ingredient kitchen produced the most instructive result of the week. In training, MORL out-cooks the baseline by 60 points playing with itself (254 vs 195). Zero-shot, it loses to the baseline against every one of the 16 strangers (97 vs 163). That is the textbook zero-shot failure — a private routine that works beautifully with its twin — and it is the strongest evidence yet that MORL's dense objectives push agents toward more specialised protocols, not more adaptable ones.

How to say it

"The multi-ingredient map is where MORL trains best and generalises worst — it out-cooks the baseline with itself and loses to it with every stranger. That's a private-protocol failure, and it tells us the dense objectives are teaching the agent a routine, not a way of reading its partner. The re-priced map, which we hoped would force role division, is simply too hard for anything to learn in the budget."

F7

Analysis code that cannot see the data looks exactly like missing data

Methods note

Four times last week, an analysis step silently discarded results while every log line looked healthy: a naming pattern that excluded hyphens, a filter that dropped valid runs, a file-name parser confused by an underscore in a map name, and an extraction step pointed at the wrong database. Each produced a plausible-looking but wrong table.

One of these was materially damaging: a de-duplication step had been discarding half our runs, and it happened to discard the better half of the baseline — putting a biased comparison underneath our headline result. Recovering those runs changed the headline number by 40%.

Worth a paragraph in the methods chapter. The general lesson: verify that analysis code sees the number of records you expect, because silently seeing fewer is indistinguishable from there being fewer. This week added three more of the same family (a training run that "completed" with an empty result, an evaluator that built the wrong game engine, an extraction pointed at the wrong project), all now fixed at the pipeline level.

F10

On the multi-ingredient kitchen there is no partner-specific optimum to adapt to

Established

We trained a dedicated best-response agent for 15 of the 16 held-out partners on unident_s_m (F4's calibration, moved from the re-priced map where nothing learns). Two results. The old "best any agent happened to manage" yardstick captures 91% of the true best response on average there — but with a 50%–130% range that reorders which partners are hard (rank correlation +0.10), the same lesson as before.

More important: the fifteen best responses are, behaviourally, the same policy (their event profiles differ by 0.008 on a scale where our agents' partner-to-partner variation is 0.18–0.25). The optimal way to play with any of these partners is one fixed routine. That is F3's "knowing your partner doesn't help" arriving from the other side: on this kitchen, adapting to the partner has no ceiling to reach. With the proper denominator, the baseline captures about 72% of what is achievable here and MORL 43%.

How to say it

"We trained a specialist for each partner on the multi-ingredient map and they all play the same way. So on that kitchen there is nothing partner-specific to adapt to — the question is just who cooks better, and the baseline does."

Two answers here, and the honest reading of each.

The partner-state hypothesis is not confirmed. F11 predicted those channels would pay once the partners varied, since stage two is the only place they can be learned from anything but a twin. They do not: the fill-in trainee scores 3.4 points below the trainee without them (p = 0.79). Combined with the stage-one ablation above, partner state is never positive beyond noise anywhere we have put it. That line is closed.

The MORL trainee is directionally the best stage-2 agent, and much more reliable. Its mean is 17 points higher, which is not significant at six runs (p = 0.45). But its worst run scores 139 against 68.8, its two worst average 144 against 85, and its spread is 3.3× tighter. I want to be careful here, because I nearly overclaimed it: an F-test on the variances gives p = 0.02, but that test assumes normality, and at six runs it is doing the work rather than the data. Distribution-free tests on the same numbers give p = 0.12 (permutation on the spread ratio), 0.098 (Levene) and 0.15 (Ansari–Bradley). So: a large, consistent direction across mean, spread and worst case — and not significant on any of them at this sample size.

Worth noting what this does not overturn: the best single agent on this kitchen remains the plain hand-shaped stage-1 agent at 170.2. No stage-2 agent in any arm, ours or the pipeline's, has beaten it. That is a finding about the benchmark's own pipeline as much as about our method.

Why, and what we are changing

Three causes, three corrected versions

The goal is still an agent that is better in these kitchens. The negative results above are specific enough to say what was wrong — and the first corrected run found a third cause the analysis had missed.

Cause 1

At the end of training, the MORL agent is being paid for the wrong thing

The hand-shaped baseline gets its dense hints (onion in pot, dish picked up) with a multiplier that is annealed to zero over training, so what it optimises at the end is deliveries alone. Our objective vector is never annealed: the agent is paid for prep and plating events forever. At convergence, deliveries are 17% of the MORL reward on unident_s and 6.5% on random0. The agent that "trains better" in self-play on the multi-ingredient map is optimising event counts, and a stranger does not need its event counts.

Fix: anneal every non-task objective the same way the baseline's shaping is annealed, so the dense objectives act as a curriculum and the terminal reward is the task. Arm bench_morl_ann. This alone should bring MORL to parity; it keeps the denser early signal and removes the late-training handicap.

Cause 2

The agent has no representation of what its partner is doing

The agent's input is the current grid: where the partner stands and what it holds. Nothing in it says what the partner has been doing this episode. A partner that has never plated looks identical to one that plates constantly. "My partner never plates, so I should" is not computable from the state we gave it — which is why every partner-adaptation check came back negative regardless of the reward. The adaptive-weights rule compounded this: it read the team's event mix and steered it toward a fixed uniform target, so it balanced the team rather than complementing the partner.

Fix: append each agent's own and its partner's episode-to-date objective mix to the observation (six extra input channels), and change the adaptation rule so each agent's weights are steered toward what its partner is doing least of — a fill-in rule. Arm bench_morl_fill (which also carries the anneal). As a control, bench_sp_shares gives the baseline reward the same partner state and nothing else, so "does seeing the partner help" is separated from everything MORL.

Cause 3

A delivery was paid to one cook; the baseline pays both

Found by the first corrected run, which converged at 195 — exactly the level of the "sparse reward only" control, and far below the baseline's 470 — even though its reward had, by then, become the task alone. The difference is who gets paid. The baseline's delivery reward is the team's: both cooks receive it. Our task objective credited a delivery only to the cook who carried it to the hatch. Once the dense objectives annealed away, the cook doing the prep was paid nothing for the soups it made possible, so each cook learned to run the whole loop alone. (This also means our "sparse-only" control was never the baseline's reward; it was a different, less cooperative one.)

Fix: credit the task to the team, exactly as the baseline does; the dense objectives stay per-cook, as the baseline's hints do. This one change moved the annealed arm from 182 to 467 in self-play (baseline 470). It is also a hypothesis about the zero-shot gap in its own right: an agent trained to be paid only for its own deliveries has every reason to deliver rather than to enable a stranger's deliveries.

Where this leaves the thesis

An honest position

The performance claim as originally stated — that multi-objective training with adaptive weights produces a better zero-shot partner — is not supported by the method as built. On the clean reward, at six runs per method, the hand-tuned baseline is better on every kitchen where agents can cook, the population pipeline makes it worse, and neither adaptive weights nor visibility of those weights helps. Those are clean negatives, and they are worth stating plainly.

What makes this a productive week rather than a bad one is that the negatives are specific. The method was optimising event counts at the end of training where the baseline optimises deliveries; it paid a delivery to one cook where the baseline pays both; and it was asked to adapt to a partner it had no state describing. The first two were real and fixing them moved the numbers. The third turned out not to be: the partner state is neutral at best, in six separate ablations, including the one where the partners actually vary. The multi-ingredient result — MORL out-cooks the baseline with itself and loses to it with every stranger — remains the clearest statement of the first cause we could have asked for.

The methodological contribution stands regardless: a reward that could be forged and a rule for spotting that class of problem in advance; a calibration that was capturing 7% of the truth; a significance test against the wrong sample; the partner-identity result on two kitchens; the best-response calibration showing that on one kitchen there is no partner-specific optimum at all; and a two-part reward diagnosis — objectives wrong at convergence, delivery credited to the wrong cook — each isolated by an ablation and each worth real points.

There is also one positive performance result, stated at the strength the data supports. Put where the proposal always said it belonged — as the trainee against a varied population — the corrected MORL agent is the best stage-2 agent we have trained, by 17 points on the mean and 70 on the worst run. Neither gap clears significance at six runs, and I would rather say that plainly than lean on the one test that reported otherwise. The recurring shape of every MORL result in this project is the same: not a higher ceiling, a higher floor.

So the thesis is about how to design and measure multi-objective rewards for coordination — an objective that could be forged and the rule that catches that class in advance, three credit-assignment failures and what each cost, a measurement apparatus that was wrong four separate ways, and a method whose honest claim is reliability rather than performance. That is a contribution that stands whether or not six more seeds move the reliability result over the line.
