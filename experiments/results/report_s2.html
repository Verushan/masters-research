MORL’s zero-shot deficit

Stage 1 showed that agents trained on the objective vector coordinate better with themselves and much worse with strangers. Population training is supposed to repair exactly that. These runs test whether it does.

Why these runs exist

The proposal replaces Overcooked’s hand-tuned shaping reward with a vector of four objectives — task completion, ingredient prep, plating, and coordination — scalarised by a weight vector w. The stage-1 benchmark asked the narrow question: can that vector replace hand-crafted shaping? It can. MORL with adaptive weights reached ~211 training return against hand-shaped self-play’s ~198, with no hand-tuned reward term anywhere.

But it also produced an uncomfortable result. Measured against held-out partners the ordering inverted: hand-shaped self-play scored 52.9, while the two MORL arms scored 12.9 and 10.4. The behavioural breakdown said why — a MORL pair performed roughly three times as many counter handoffs as a hand-shaped pair. The coordination objective did exactly what it was asked to do and produced a high-throughput handoff protocol. But a tightly-timed protocol is a convention, and a partner who does not share it cannot join.

That is a real finding, but it is not a verdict, because stage 1 only ever trains agents against themselves. Self-play on any reward converges to a private convention; a reward that pays for coordination converges to a tighter one. Repairing that is the entire purpose of the second stage of the pipeline, which trains a best-response agent against a frozen population of diverse partners.

The question these runs answer

Stage 1 measured what each reward produces in self-play. It could not say what the pipeline produces when the population it trains against was raised on the objective vector. If FCP closes the gap, MORL’s zero-shot deficit is a stage-1 artifact and the reward design stands. If the gap survives, reward design and partner diversity are addressing different failures — and the MORL component cannot substitute for the population component.

The experiment

One stage-2 run per stage-1 population. Each population is that arm’s three seeds at init/mid/final — nine partners — so the arms are matched on population size and differ only in the reward those partners optimised.

Two controls make the comparison readable. First, the ego agent optimises the task reward in every arm; giving the MORL arms a MORL ego reward as well would confound “MORL population” with “MORL ego” and neither result could be attributed. Second, --morl_objectives default is passed to every arm, including the ones whose reward it does not touch, so all four report the same behavioural breakdown.

Self-play and ZSC are deterministic; stability is the standard deviation across repeated stochastic rollouts of the same pair. BR-Prox* is a proxy — the denominator is the best score any policy in the pool achieved with that partner, which is a lower bound on a true best response, so the proxy is optimistic.

Stage 2 helps every arm. In relative terms it helps the MORL arms most — roughly threefold, against hand-shaped SP’s 41%. That is the headline a generous reading would take.

The absolute numbers say something less generous. The gain is near-constant across all three arms: +21.5, +23.3, +19.6. FCP adds a fixed increment; it does not repair the deficit. s2_bench_sp still beats both MORL arms by roughly 2×, which is the stage-1 ordering intact. Every supporting metric agrees — BR-Prox* runs 0.50 / 0.30 / 0.20 in the same order.

Not fixed by any arm

Worst-case zero-shot return is 0.0 for every group in the pool, including s2_bench_sp and the mixed population. On forced coordination, every agent here still fails completely with at least one held-out partner. Nothing in this experiment improves the worst case — only the mean.

The mixed population is the result worth following

The agent trained against bench_sp + bench_morl_ad together reaches 71.2 — statistically indistinguishable from the SP-only population’s 74.4, from a population that is half MORL agents. Reward diversity costs nothing here when it is added to task-shaped partners rather than replacing them.

The caveat is structural and cannot be argued away in this run: mixed has 18 partners against everyone else’s 9. Population size and reward diversity are confounded, and a larger population is independently expected to help. This is a hypothesis for a size-matched follow-up — nine partners drawn half from each arm — not yet a finding.

The agent trained on the uniform-weight population barely functions — one delivery, fourteen handoffs, and a deterministic self-play score of 20.0. That number needs a caveat rather than an interpretation: its population contains the stage-1 seed that collapsed to zero deliveries at ~1.05M steps under uniform weights, so roughly a third of what it trained against never delivers a soup. It reflects a broken population at least as much as a MORL one, and the 36.2 vs 30.0 ordering between the two MORL arms should not be read as meaningful.

What this means for the proposal

  1. Population training does not substitute for reward design. The stage-1 report left open the possibility that FCP would repair MORL’s brittleness. It improves it and preserves the ranking. The two components address different failures, and this is an argument for combining them — which is what the proposal already plans.
  2. A MORL population is not a drop-in replacement for a task-shaped one, but it appears to compose with one at no cost. That is the most actionable direction here, and it is cheap to test properly.
  3. Worst-case ZSC is untouched. Every group scores 0.0 against its worst partner. If the proposal cares about worst-case coordination rather than mean, nothing measured here moves it, and a different mechanism will be needed.
  4. The next run should be size-matched on the mixed arm. Nine partners drawn half from bench_sp and half from bench_morl_ad separates reward diversity from population size, which is the one question this pilot raised and could not answer.

How much to trust this

These are pilot numbers, and the honest reading is directional.

  • Two seeds at 1e6 steps — half the budget morl-s2.slurm defaults to. On random0, stage 1 already showed a single seed swinging the fixed-weight result, so the MORL-arm ordering is within noise.
  • One layout, and the one most likely to flatter the coordination objective. random0 is forced coordination — a counter column separates the two cooks, so every ingredient must be handed across. A non-partitioned layout is the obvious replication.
  • The evaluation partners are held-out self-play checkpoints, not ZSC-Eval’s own HSP bias agents, so they are less behaviourally diverse than the benchmark intends.
  • Nine-partner populations against the pipeline’s usual 48. The existing fcp-S2 agent at 13.5 was trained for 500k steps against a mixed-quality pool and should not anchor anything.
